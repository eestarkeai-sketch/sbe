The Hidden Bottlenecks Slowing Your Business Down

Where is the work waiting?

Ethan Starke

Executive Advisor  |  Author  | 
Speaker  |  Entrepreneur

Most businesses do not struggle because of a lack of effort. They struggle because effort gets trapped, in places you cannot see from inside the day. Those places are bottlenecks.

The ask

Remove one bottleneck this week.

Not a plan to remove it. The actual removal.

Hand off the decision. Stop the activity. Name the owner. Block the time.

Then, next week, do it again with the next one. That is how a business gets faster without getting bigger.

Start here

Three questions

You do not need to answer all three. Answer the one that creates the strongest recognition or discomfort. Then circle it.

  1. What decision is waiting on me right now that someone else could make?
  2. What are we doing that we should stop?
  3. Where does work wait longest in my business?

What we are looking for

Four hidden bottlenecks

None of them shows up on a profit and loss statement. All of them show up in your week.

1

You

Everything routes through one person.
  • People wait for you.
  • Work stops when you are out.
  • You answer the same question again.

Sometimes the business genuinely needs us at the center. Sometimes we have simply become accustomed to being needed.

First moveList every decision that waited on you last week. You may be surprised how many someone else could have made with a little context.

2

Everything is urgent

Every opportunity feels equally urgent.
  • The list grows faster than it shrinks.
  • Ten things worked, two finished.
  • New ideas arrive before old ones are done.

First moveName your top three priorities for this quarter, out loud, to your team. Then name three things you are deliberately not doing. The second list is the one that creates focus.

3

Nobody clearly owns it

Work bounces, and nobody finishes it.
  • Two people each assumed the other had it.
  • Decisions made in hallways, never written down.
  • Pressure applied where ownership is missing.

First moveFor anything that keeps bouncing, write one name next to it. One person, not a department. When everyone owns something, usually nobody does.

4

Your attention

What you attend to becomes important to everyone else.
  • A full day you will not remember in a month.
  • The important project still says “next week”.
  • Distraction: a slow corrosion, not a dramatic failure.

First moveLook at last week’s calendar and mark each block that was work only you could do. Count them.

Better decisions

Is it easy to undo?

Every bottleneck is, underneath, a decision problem. Not every decision deserves the same care. Sort decisions by two questions.

How much is at stake? How easily can we undo it?

Low stakes
Easy to undo

Let someone else decide

High stakes
Easy to undo

Try it, review it early

Low stakes
Hard to undo

Decide once, write down why

High stakes
Hard to undo

Slow down on purpose

Most owners have this backwards. They agonize over small decisions they could undo tomorrow, and rush the big ones because the big ones feel stressful.

Speed without clarity is recklessness disguised as momentum.

The Stoic Entrepreneur

Write down what you are assuming.

Before a significant decision, write down the two or three things that must be true for it to work. Now you know exactly what to watch. If one of them turns out to be false, you can change course early.

A sensible delay has a date and a reason. Avoidance has neither.

Share the reasoning, not just the verdict.

When people routinely hear the reasoning behind decisions, they begin to think with the same lens. Share the reasoning and you are training the next ten decisions, not just making this one.

The clarity of a decision is rarely found in the outcome; it is found in the reasoning that shaped it.

Start any of these on Monday

Four moves

  1. Stop one thing.

    An activity, a product, a report or a meeting that takes effort and no longer earns its place. Look for the thing everyone quietly dreads, or the report nobody reads.

  2. Hand off one decision, with the reasoning.

    Explain how you make it, what you look for, and where the limits are. Then let them make it. Start with a small, reversible one.

  3. Protect one block of focus.

    One uninterrupted block each week for the work only you can do. Put it on the calendar before the week starts, not after it fills.

  4. Set a short weekly rhythm.

    Fifteen minutes, same time every week. What is waiting? Who owns it? What are we stopping?

None of these requires a new hire. None requires more hours. Your business already knows where the work is waiting. The job is to look.
